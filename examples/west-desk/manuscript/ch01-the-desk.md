# April 2001

## The room

The west desk was four chairs at the end of a long floor, and the fourth
chair was mine. I had come over from settlements in the first week of
April, which is the kind of transfer nobody announces: a box, a badge that
still opened the wrong doors, and a note from Marcus Idowu that said
"sit here" with an arrow drawn on it.

Marcus ran the desk. He had run it since before the tie was built, and he
spoke about the operator the way other people speak about weather, as a
thing you plan around and never argue with. His rule, the first one I
learned, was that the desk did not schedule what it could not deliver.
It sounds obvious written down. It was not obvious at eleven in the morning
with a trader shouting from the next floor.

Priya Venkataraman sat opposite. She traded real-time and kept the tie's
figures the way some people keep a diary: hourly, in her own hand, on the
back of the previous day's awards. Everyone called her PV, including the
operator, and her email signature was two letters and nothing else.

Tomas Lindqvist scheduled. He was the calmest person on the floor, which on
a power desk is a professional qualification, and he had a checklist he
closed every morning at ten-thirty as if the building depended on it. It
did, some mornings.

Corinne Baptiste was not at the desk but belonged to it. She sat with the
analysts one floor down and reconciled what we scheduled against what the
operator said we had scheduled, which were never quite the same numbers.
Half of what I know about that year I know because Corinne wrote it down.

## What the desk did

The Cascade tie was the reason the desk existed. It was one line, or the
operator treated it as one line, and it joined our side of the map to the
northwest, where Wes Hargrove ran the Portland office and told us every
week what the river was doing. Power went north in the winter and south in
the summer, and the desk's whole job was to schedule that flow an hour at a
time without promising the operator something the line could not carry.

The operator posted a rating for the tie every morning. Priya pulled the
posting before she did anything else, before coffee, and if the rating had
moved she said so out loud to the room. A derate, a cut to the rating, meant
everything scheduled across the tie for that day was suspect until somebody
had done the arithmetic again. In April nothing moved. I remember thinking
the desk was quiet. Marcus told me, when I said so, that April was always
quiet, and that I should learn the checklist while it was.

## The checklist

Tomas's checklist had eleven lines. I never saw the eleventh line used;
Tomas said it was there for the day the first ten were not enough. The
first three were the operator's postings, the derate line and the day-ahead
awards, in that order, and if the three disagreed with each other the
checklist stopped and Marcus was told.

I learned it in a week, the way you learn a route by walking it, and by the
end of April I was closing it with Tomas at ten-thirty and reading the
figures back to Priya. That was the whole apprenticeship. There was no
course. There was a desk, a rating, a cutoff at noon, and three people who
had done it long enough to know which of those things would go wrong first.

It was the cutoff. But that was May.
