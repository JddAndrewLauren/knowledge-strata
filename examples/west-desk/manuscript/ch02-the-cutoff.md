# The week of 16 May 2001

## The eleven o'clock rule

The note came on a Wednesday afternoon, from Marcus to the desk, three
lines long. Starting Monday the scheduling cutoff moved to eleven. Noon
submissions would be refused. That was all it said, and it was all it
needed to say, because everybody on the desk understood at once that an
hour had just been taken out of the only part of the morning that had any
slack in it.

Tomas answered the next morning at eight. He had already moved the
checklist to ten-thirty, he said, so that the eleven o'clock cutoff had
half an hour of slack. I remember being surprised that he had done it
overnight, and then not surprised, because that was Tomas. The checklist
lived in his head before it lived on paper, and the paper moved when he
did.

The reason for the change was never written down, and I have looked. The
operator wanted the hour; Marcus gave it to them; the desk absorbed it.
Later, when the tie went out and the settlement statements came back
wrong, the eleven o'clock rule was the reason we had a defensible record of
what we had scheduled and when. Nobody said so at the time. It was just an
hour that went missing from a Wednesday.

## Morning

The first Monday under the new rule I was at the desk at six. Priya was
already there, and had been, I think, since five, because the posting had
moved and she wanted the arithmetic done before Tomas arrived. The rating
was down a fraction. Not a derate, she said, just a fraction, and then she
did the arithmetic anyway.

By ten-thirty the checklist was closed. By ten-fifty the schedule was in.
Tomas leaned back in his chair for the first time I had ever seen him do
it, and the desk had ten minutes it had not had the week before, and we
spent them saying nothing at all.

## The working group

The congestion working group met at the end of the month, on the thirtieth,
at ten. Priya's note about it said to bring the hourly figures for the tie
and not the daily roll-up, which was her way of saying the roll-up hid the
thing she wanted the group to see. She sent the load figures the week
before, as an attachment nobody on the desk could open without asking her
to, and then she brought a paper copy, which is what we actually used.

What the figures showed was that the tie was running near its rating for
three hours every afternoon and near nothing the rest of the day, and that
the daily average, the number the operator quoted, was therefore a lie of
the honest kind. Marcus took the paper copy into the meeting. He came back
with it folded in half and said the group would meet again in June.

## Morning

The second heading called Morning is here on purpose. A memoir has more
than one morning in it, and a reader who cites this chapter by heading
will have to say which one she means. This is the one after the working
group: the last day of May, the rating unchanged, the checklist closed at
ten-thirty, and Priya reading the afternoon peak back to the room from the
back of yesterday's awards. It was the quietest morning of the year. I did
not know it then.

## What June was going to be

The Portland call was the first Monday of June. Wes wanted the whole desk
on the line at nine Central, which meant seven his time, which meant Wes
had been up since five to tell us about the river. The Friday schedule
moved to after the call. Marcus signed off on it in the reply chain, in
the way he signed off on everything, by not objecting.

The river was high. That was the message. Power was going to go south all
summer, across the tie, at the rating, every afternoon. It was going to be
the busiest summer the desk had ever had, Wes said, and he was right about
that, though not for the reason he thought.
